Seamless Kubernetes Rollouts: Probes, Graceful Shutdown, and PDB

Verifiable zero-downtime rollouts: startup, readiness, and liveness probes, Spring Boot graceful shutdown, Deployment strategy, preStop, and PDB.

A few Connection refused or Connect timed out errors during a rolling rollout should not be written off as “normal.” They usually signal that the new Pod started taking traffic too early, the old Pod exited too quickly, the application failed to shut down gracefully, or the workload actually had only one available replica.

What Happens During a Pod Replacement

A seamless rollout hinges on the entire sequence, not on any single YAML parameter.

The Three Probes Each Own One Job

According to the official Kubernetes docs, once startupProbe is configured, readiness and liveness do not run until it succeeds. The Liveness, Readiness, and Startup Probes page is the baseline for designing probes.

startupProbe: Has the Application Finished Initializing

Good for services with slow Spring context initialization, database migrations, or cache warm-up:

startupProbe:
  httpGet:
    path: /actuator/health/readiness
    port: management
  periodSeconds: 10
  timeoutSeconds: 5
  failureThreshold: 30

The maximum tolerated startup time is roughly:

initialDelaySeconds + periodSeconds × failureThreshold

The example above works out to about 300 seconds. timeoutSeconds is the timeout for a single probe attempt and should not simply be multiplied into the total.

readinessProbe: Can It Accept Requests Right Now

readinessProbe:
  httpGet:
    path: /actuator/health/readiness
    port: management
  periodSeconds: 10
  timeoutSeconds: 5
  failureThreshold: 3
  successThreshold: 1

A failed readiness probe does not restart the container; it only removes the Pod from the Service’s EndpointSlice. It’s fine for readiness to fail when the database connection pool is exhausted or a required dependency is unavailable, but don’t let one degradable dependency take the whole workload into NotReady.

livenessProbe: Has the Process Lost Its Ability to Self-Heal

livenessProbe:
  httpGet:
    path: /actuator/health/liveness
    port: management
  periodSeconds: 10
  timeoutSeconds: 5
  failureThreshold: 12

10/5/12 means a probe fires every 10 seconds, waits at most 5 seconds per attempt, and restarts the container after 12 consecutive failures. The rough failure window is about 120 seconds, not 60.

Don’t let liveness reach deep into the database, Redis, Nacos, or external HTTP. When a dependency blocks, liveness restarts every Pod, which can turn a local failure into a restart storm.

Get Spring Boot Graceful Shutdown Right First

server.shutdown=graceful
spring.lifecycle.timeout-per-shutdown-phase=30s

After receiving SIGTERM, the application should:

  1. Stop accepting new requests.
  2. Wait for in-flight requests to finish.
  3. Stop message listeners and scheduled tasks.
  4. Commit or roll back transactions.
  5. Close connection pools and exit.

Verify it with logs rather than trusting the config:

kubectl -n <namespace> delete pod <test-pod>
kubectl -n <namespace> logs <test-pod> -f

terminationGracePeriodSeconds Is the Total Budget

spec:
  terminationGracePeriodSeconds: 120

It covers the combined time for preStop, graceful application shutdown, and container exit. Once the budget is exceeded, kubelet force-terminates the process.

A value of 120 seconds does not mean every deletion has to wait 120 seconds; if the process exits early, the Pod ends immediately.

If the worst-case request takes 45 seconds, preStop waits 10 seconds, and stopping the message listener takes 20 seconds, a 60-second total budget may be too short.

preStop Is for Draining, Not for Masking a Slow Startup

The simplest approach:

lifecycle:
  preStop:
    exec:
      command: ["sh", "-c", "sleep 10"]

This buys EndpointSlice propagation and upstream connection pools a little draining time, but those 10 seconds count toward the grace period.

More mature applications can take these steps proactively in the preStop hook:

  • Set readiness to false.
  • Stop pulling new messages from Kafka/RabbitMQ.
  • Wait for current tasks to complete.

Do not replace a proper application lifecycle with a fixed 180-second sleep.

The Deployment Strategy Determines How Many Pods Are Available at Once

spec:
  replicas: 2
  minReadySeconds: 15
  progressDeadlineSeconds: 600
  strategy:
    type: RollingUpdate
    rollingUpdate:
      maxSurge: 1
      maxUnavailable: 0

Meaning:

  • A rollout is allowed to create one extra Pod temporarily.
  • Existing available replicas cannot be reduced until the new Pod is Ready.
  • After Ready, the new Pod must stay healthy for 15 seconds before it counts as available.

This assumes the node pool has room for the surge Pod. When resources are full, the new Pod stays Pending and the rollout stalls, while the old service keeps running.

A single replica cannot survive node failure even with maxUnavailable: 0, and applications using ReadWriteOnce local volumes may not be able to start the new and old Pods at the same time.

PDB Is Not a Deployment Rollout Strategy

apiVersion: policy/v1
kind: PodDisruptionBudget
metadata:
  name: app-api
spec:
  minAvailable: 1
  selector:
    matchLabels:
      app.kubernetes.io/name: app-api

PDB mainly constrains voluntary disruptions that go through the Eviction API, such as kubectl drain. The Kubernetes docs are explicit: the rolling updates of Deployments and StatefulSets themselves are not subject to PDB; they are governed by the workload’s strategy. Disruptions

So all of the following are needed:

  • maxSurge/maxUnavailable on the Deployment.
  • A PDB to protect node maintenance.
  • Anti-affinity or topology spread to keep replicas off the same node.

A Complete Template

apiVersion: apps/v1
kind: Deployment
metadata:
  name: app-api
spec:
  replicas: 2
  minReadySeconds: 15
  progressDeadlineSeconds: 600
  strategy:
    rollingUpdate: { maxSurge: 1, maxUnavailable: 0 }
  template:
    spec:
      terminationGracePeriodSeconds: 120
      containers:
        - name: app
          image: <image-registry>/app-api:<version>@sha256:<digest>
          ports:
            - { name: management, containerPort: 8081 }
          lifecycle:
            preStop:
              exec: { command: ["sh", "-c", "sleep 10"] }
          startupProbe:
            httpGet: { path: /actuator/health/readiness, port: management }
            periodSeconds: 10
            timeoutSeconds: 5
            failureThreshold: 30
          readinessProbe:
            httpGet: { path: /actuator/health/readiness, port: management }
            periodSeconds: 10
            timeoutSeconds: 5
            failureThreshold: 3
          livenessProbe:
            httpGet: { path: /actuator/health/liveness, port: management }
            periodSeconds: 10
            timeoutSeconds: 5
            failureThreshold: 12

Do not copy these parameters blindly. Calculate them from the application’s real startup-time percentiles, request latency, message-processing time, and dependency recovery time.

If dozens of services will reuse this rollout structure, check out the unified Spring Boot Deployment template: it keeps one template source and expresses resource tiers, node pools, and legacy-app compatibility exceptions as parameters.

Check Capacity Before Releasing

kubectl -n <namespace> get deployment app-api -o wide
kubectl describe node <target-node> | sed -n '/Allocated resources:/,$p'
kubectl get events -A --field-selector reason=FailedScheduling

A rollout needs enough headroom for one extra Pod’s worth of requests. Don’t mistake the low utilization shown by a current kubectl top for available scheduler capacity.

How to Verify It Is Truly Seamless

Send requests continuously from a separate terminal:

while true; do
  curl -fsS -o /dev/null -w '%{http_code} %{time_total}\n' \
    https://app.example.com/<business-read-api>
  sleep 0.2
done

Start the rollout:

kubectl -n <namespace> set image deployment/app-api \
  app=<image-registry>/app-api:<new-version>@sha256:<new-digest>

kubectl -n <namespace> rollout status deployment/app-api --timeout=15m
kubectl -n <namespace> get pod -l app.kubernetes.io/name=app-api -w

Watch in parallel:

kubectl -n <namespace> get endpointslice \
  -l kubernetes.io/service-name=app-api -w

kubectl -n <namespace> logs deployment/app-api --since=15m \
  | grep -Ei 'timeout|refused|shutdown|killed|oom'

Acceptance requires more than HTTP 200: also check the error rate, P95/P99, message backlog, database connections, and thread pools.

Common Mistakes

SymptomRoot causeFix direction
New Pod errors right after Readyreadiness is too shallow; dependencies not readyMove genuinely required initialization into the readiness check
Old Pod exits while still servinggrace period too short or no graceful shutdownExtend the budget and verify SIGTERM
All Pods restart at onceliveness depends deeply on shared middlewareliveness only judges whether the process can self-heal
New Pod stays Pendingno surge capacityFree up or add node capacity
Rollout interrupted despite PDBPDB does not control Deployment rolloutsConfigure the rollout strategy and replica count

Definition of Done

  • At least two available replicas spread across different nodes.
  • startup, readiness, and liveness each have exactly one responsibility.
  • The application exits within the grace period after receiving SIGTERM.
  • maxUnavailable: 0, with surge capacity on the nodes.
  • The PDB can block unsafe node drains.
  • Continuous traffic during the rollout shows no 5xx, connection refusals, or abnormal timeouts.
  • A failed rollout can be restored to the previous Revision with kubectl rollout undo.

A seamless rollout is not about cranking up all three probes; it’s about keeping traffic intake, traffic draining, and the process lifecycle strictly aligned.
